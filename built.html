What I've built

Klaviyo segmentation, flow architecture and flow logic for eleven DTC and e-commerce brands.

01

Health & wellness brand

£11.8M email revenue over 31 months

Result: Email grew from ~17% to over 50% of total store revenue at peak, and held 35–47% two and a half years in.

Brand: High-ticket health & wellness DTC brand, large and fast-growing list.

Starting state

  • Only default Klaviyo flows: a basic welcome and a cart abandonment sequence
  • Campaigns sent to the full list, no meaningful segmentation
  • List growing faster than engagement; 364 spam complaints in the first month

What I built

  1. Flow architecture, 15–20 flows from scratch: welcome, browse abandonment, cart abandonment, post-purchase, cross-sell/upsell, replenishment, win-back, VIP nurture and sunset.
  2. Flow logic: win-back staged at 60, 90 and 120 days since last order with graduated offers; replenishment timed to average product lifespan; cross-sell recommendations driven by purchase history; incentives in cart recovery reserved for high-value carts.
  3. Segmentation for campaigns: audiences split by engagement, purchase history and product interest, so campaigns scaled from 911K to 12M+ monthly recipients without blasting the whole file.
  4. Peak-season segmentation: a VIP segment received early access 48 hours before the main list, with cart, browse and re-engagement flows running alongside campaigns.

The deliverability crisis: the cleanup

Scaling that fast brought dead weight. Spam complaints hit 11,392 in a single month. I rebuilt the sending setup around list health:

  1. Suppressed anyone with no open or click in 90 days from active sending
  2. Cleaned the file of hard bounces, spam traps, role-based and disposable addresses
  3. Sent only to the most engaged segments in small batches for several weeks to rebuild sender reputation
  4. Put permanent hygiene in place: an automated sunset flow, double opt-in on high-risk signup sources and monthly cleaning

Open rate rose from the low-to-mid 20s to 39.54% within four months, and revenue per subscriber went up on a smaller list.

02

Long-established food brand

Reviving a dormant list with 14 segments and 9 flows

Result: A list that hadn't been emailed properly in over a year returned a 41.6% open rate and 7.56% click rate on its first reactivation send, with zero spam complaints.

Brand: Decades-old clean-ingredient food brand that lost customers after a product change, then reversed it without telling them.

Starting state

  • ~16,000 customers; under 10% engaged in the last 90 days
  • 8,608 customers had received no email in 365 days
  • 3,715 lapsed customers tied to the product change
  • No flows at all: no welcome, post-purchase, abandonment, win-back or sunset

The technical problem: Blasting a cold list would have triggered complaints and bounces and damaged the sending domain. Infrastructure had to come before any campaign.

What I built

  1. 14 custom segments in four groups: campaign send pools, customer recovery pools, suppression and risk segments, and reusable exclusion audiences.
  2. Account-wide engagement rules: no segment relied on Apple Mail Privacy opens; engagement was defined by clicks, purchases and real site activity.
  3. Send safeguards: suppression applied before every send, Smart Sending on globally, and cold audiences released in controlled batches across warm-then-cold waves.
  4. 9 lifecycle flows, 40+ emails: welcome, first-time and repeat post-purchase, abandoned checkout, abandoned cart, browse abandonment, win-back, replenishment, cross-sell and sunset.
  5. Flow logic: replenishment timed to realistic consumption windows rather than fixed dates; cross-sell flavour recommendations switched dynamically on previous purchases; discounts held back to the final touchpoint; conditional splits by lifecycle stage and subscription status.

Results: first reactivation send (5,633 recipients, 24 hours)

Click rate7.56%
Orders13
Spam complaints0

My role: Segment architecture, suppression logic, deliverability setup and all nine flow builds. Copy direction and campaign messaging by the team.

03

Premium pet brand

£667K attributed revenue, flows as the backbone

Result: Attributed revenue grew 65.6% YoY to £667K, with flows growing fastest (+79.9%) and producing 55% of revenue from 5% of the recipient volume.

Brand: Premium pet products, scaling quickly. Klaviyo drove 41.5% of total store revenue in the period.

Starting state: Revenue was growing, but efficiency was slipping. The list expanded aggressively while clicks stayed flat and revenue per recipient fell. Several high-performing flows weren't fully live.

What I built

  1. Flow-first architecture: welcome, abandoned checkout, abandoned cart, browse abandonment and post-purchase treated as the core revenue engine, with campaigns as the reach layer.
  2. SMS launch: SMS added only at high-intent trigger points, cart abandonment, post-purchase and low-inventory alerts, rather than as a broadcast channel.
  3. Reporting clarity at scale: flow-by-flow and channel-level revenue tracking so it stayed clear which assets carried revenue as recipient volume grew 230%.
  4. Rollout audit: identified three abandonment flows still in Draft (including a third-party identity-resolution browse flow) that had already produced £121K, a ready roadmap of untapped revenue.

04

Community apparel brand

220% growth in attributed revenue

Result: Revenue doubled in year one; in year two, Klaviyo-attributed revenue rose 220.6% to £719K on £1.61M total revenue.

Brand: Long-running apparel retailer serving a large membership organisation. Customers buy around the organisation's own events, not retail holidays.

Starting state: No segmentation and no SMS. Campaigns went to the full list on a loose schedule. Twenty years of purchase history and event-driven buying patterns sat unused.

What I built

  1. Event-calendar segmentation: segments built from purchase history and event-linked buying, so each subscriber received campaigns for the events relevant to them, not every event.
  2. Multi-stage event sequences: teaser, countdown, day-of and post-event follow-up phases for each major event, each targeted to its own segment.
  3. SMS channel from zero: opt-in setup and an SMS audience reserved for limited drops, early access, low-inventory alerts and event-day reminders.
  4. Flow rebuild: welcome, browse abandonment, post-purchase cross-sell, and win-back triggered by each customer's typical repurchase cycle.
  5. Measurement loop: revenue by channel, campaign vs flow, click-through by segment and conversion by product category and event, used to tighten segments every week.

Results

05

Spirits & wine retailer

296.9% growth in the industry's slowest quarter

Result: In Q1, the quarter liquor retail usually writes off, attributed revenue rose 296.9% YoY to £77K, with flow revenue up 328%.

Brand: Online spirits and wine retailer with a large catalogue, from everyday staples to allocated bottles.

Starting state: One-size-fits-all campaigns. A whiskey collector, a wine buyer and a tequila fan got the same send. Effort was concentrated in Q4, with almost nothing in Q1.

What I built

  1. Preference segmentation: segments from purchase and browse behaviour, whiskey collectors, wine enthusiasts, tequila, vodka, craft-spirit explorers, and seasonal gifters active only in Nov–Dec.
  2. Segment-specific content routing: each segment received its own recommendations, e.g. limited-release alerts for collectors who had bought several bottles of a category in six months.
  3. SMS restricted to urgency: flash sales, allocated-stock alerts, delivery windows and holiday countdowns only, keeping opt-outs low.
  4. Holiday browse-triggered flows to catch gift shoppers during Q4.
  5. Testing loop: A/B tests on subject lines, send times, product positioning and offer structure; winning patterns from one segment tested on others.

06

Home furnishings brand

153.6% growth in attributed revenue

Result: Total revenue rose 109.4% to £1.30M in year one; attributed revenue grew 153.6% YoY to £681K in year two.

Brand: Home furnishings with a wide catalogue and a long consideration cycle, customers browse, measure and compare for weeks.

Starting state: Every subscriber got the same email on a fixed Tuesday/Thursday calendar. Purchase history, browse data, category interest and RFM signals were all unused.

What I built

  1. Behavioural segmentation: segments by home type, budget range, product category interest and browsing intensity, separating apartment dwellers from homeowners, first-time buyers from redecorators, storage-focused from design-focused shoppers.
  2. Email + SMS cart choreography: cart abandonment sends email first, then an SMS 24 hours later; SMS otherwise reserved for flash sales, back-in-stock and limited-time offers.
  3. Consideration-cycle flows: longer, patient browse abandonment sequences, and post-purchase flows recommending the next piece for the same room rather than a random upsell.
  4. Analytics loop: conversion value by flow, revenue per recipient, opens by segment and clicks by category tracked weekly and fed back into segment and timing changes.

09

Multi-event organiser

£28K from one funnel, 4,153 registrations

Result: Connected behavioural flows and tagged funnels turned four separate events into one system: £27,800 from a single funnel and 4,153+ tagged registrations.

Client: Large annual expo running a luncheon, sessions, a tradeshow and a concert.

Starting state: Each event had its own emails, landing page and form, with no shared data. Flows were minimal and didn't capture intent.

What I built

  1. Behavioural trigger flows: page-visit, cart abandonment, and cross-event flows that fired when someone registered for one event but not another.
  2. Interest tagging: every registrant tagged by event and interest in Klaviyo, so low-ticket and free registrations became warm segments for higher-ticket events.
  3. Funnel-to-Klaviyo wiring for four funnels with distinct jobs: a high-ticket luncheon, packaged session passes, a low-ticket entry event, and a free concert built for volume.
  4. Workflow campaigns: automated, behaviour-timed sequences in place of calendar broadcasts.
